Scientific Machine Learning for Physics

In classical statistical learning, we minimize empirical risk over independent and identically distributed draws from an unknown distribution. Hence, all structure must be inferred from this finite set of samples, and the central tension becomes balancing estimation error versus approximation error (i.e., bias–variance / making overly simple assumptions or missing key patterns vs. a model being too sensitive to small fluctuations, underfitting vs. overfitting).

In physics, when describing the governing dynamics of a system, e.g., heat diffusing in a rod or, more recently, the flow of (in/compressible) fluids, we commonly use a system of equations to capture these dynamics. Therefore, in Scientific Machine Learning, the data-generating mechanism is (at least partly) known in closed form. This allows for domain knowledge to seep into the construction of models, such as

  • Supervision to come from the equation itself (most notably the physics-informed loss [0] [1] [2]), not only from observed pairs
  • Prior knowledge can be used as an inductive bias via, e.g., losses capturing conservation laws [3] [4], symmetries [5], boundary conditions, or on the architecture [6], restricting the hypothesis class to only physically admissible functions

Unlike general-purpose machine learning, scientific machine learning often aims to extrapolate to new parameters or longer time horizons, or to solve ill-posed initial-condition and coefficient-recovery problems, rather than interpolate within a stationary distribution.

However, these tasks depend heavily on how much of this mechanism is known. For this, we first need to introduce a general description of our system and how we observe it.

01

The System

For physics at the continuum scale, where the state of a system is described by fields such as velocity, temperature, or displacement, most problems can be framed as a system of partial differential equations describing the dynamics of the state.

The operator, its coefficients, the forcing, the domain and the boundary conditions define the system; together with one initial condition u0​, it becomes an instance with a single solution.

Many tasks work in discrete time, on states ut​ at time steps t=0,1,… spaced by Δt. The system then advances the state by its solution map over one step, ut+1​=M(ut​), and Mt​ denotes the map from u0​ to ut​. A learned approximation of this one-step map is written Φ (or Φθ​ with weights θ).

A simple example for such a system is the heat diffusion in a rod, whose temperature u(x,t) evolves as

02

The Observation Model

The system describes how the world behaves; however, observations of the state u are rarely exact. Instead, what we have is an observation model, which describes on one hand what our sensors capture and on the other the errors they introduce into our measurements.

y=H(u)+ε,

Observation

H
observation operatormaps the full state to what the sensors record, e.g., point values H(u)=(u(xi​,tj​))i,j​, local averages, or only some components of u
y
observations, datawhat is actually measured, such as sensor readings, images or satellite data; typically sparse, partial and indirect
ε
measurement noisesensor and modeling errors, often assumed Gaussian, ε∼N(0,Γ) with covariance Γ

H is our observation model, reading the temperature of our heat rod system from before at a few sensor positions and times. As with real sensors, each reading carries a measurement error ε.

03

The Tasks

From this perspective, we define a task by what we know about the problem's parts and by the form, quality, and quantity of the prediction we want. Most tasks, from simulating a flow to inferring a system property, are then a question of one side given the other; i.e., predicting observations from a known system, or inferring the system from observations. That is, tasks differ only in what is known and what is sought.
