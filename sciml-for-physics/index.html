Scientific Machine Learning for Physics

General-purpose machine learning (ML) models make only generic assumptions about their data (e.g., smoothness, locality, ...). Scientific machine learning (SciML) uses the very same methods but trades some of this generality by introducing domain knowledge. In physics, such domain knowledge can be highly specific, including a system's symmetries or its governing equations. It can enter the process at different places, for example in the architecture [0] or as residuals in the training signal [1] [2] [3].

01

The System

For physics at the continuum scale, where the state of a system is described by fields such as velocity, temperature, or displacement, most problems can be framed as a set of systems describing the dynamics of the system.

State

u
state (solution field)the quantity the physics determines, u:Ω×[0,T]→Rm, e.g., temperature, velocity, pressure or concentration; in ML often what a network is trained to approximate

Physics

N
(differential) operatorthe encoding the governing dynamics in the physical system, e.g., heat diffusion N[u;λ]=∂t​u−αΔu; for time-dependent problems it defines how the state evolves, where every admissible u must satisfy it at every point
λ
coefficients, physical parametersknobs on the properties of the system, such as diffusivity, viscosity or permeability; changing them can change the dynamics observed, e.g., flow stays laminar below a certain Reynolds number and turns turbulent above it
f
source, forcing terman external driving force, such as a heat source, a body force or an injection well; with f=0 the system evolves only through its own dynamics

Domain and conditions

Ω, (0,T]
domain and time horizonΩ⊂Rd is the region in which the system acts, including its geometry (irregular shapes, holes, moving parts); its boundary ∂Ω is where the system interacts with its surroundings
B, b
boundary operator and constraintB sets the type of condition: Dirichlet B[u]=u (value), Neumann B[u]=∂n​u (flux) or Robin B[u]=αu+β∂n​u (a mix); b holds the values imposed, e.g., a wall held at 20∘C or zero flux through an insulated wall
u0​

The operator, its coefficients, the forcing, the domain and the boundary conditions define the system; together with one initial condition u0​, it becomes an instance with a single solution.

02

The Observation Model

The system describes how the world behaves; however, observations of the state u are rarely exact. Instead, what we have is an observation model, modeling on one hand. e.g., what our sensor capture and on the other the measurement errors these introduce into our measurements.

y=H(u)+ε,

Observation

H
observation operatormaps the full state to what the sensors record, e.g., point values H(u)=(u(xi​,tj​))i,j​, local averages, or only some components of u
y
observations, datawhat is actually measured, such as sensor readings, images or satellite data; typically sparse, partial and indirect
ε
measurement noisesensor and modeling errors, often assumed Gaussian, ε∼N(0,Γ) with covariance Γ

H is our observation model, reading the temperature of our heat rod system from before at a few sensor positions at a few sample times. As with true sensors, we get some measurement error ε, which is sampled by some selectable distribution.
